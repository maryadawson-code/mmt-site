Confidence: High on the standards, the GAO findings, the executive order, and the contract notices as posted. Medium on the pilot descriptions, which combine SAM.gov notices with a builder's self-report. Low on anything described as current status for PARATUS, the joint SHA, or the pilots' production path; all three are open checks. Analytical on the decision-window read and every owner assignment.

The lead finding for a capture team: every one of the six lanes has a decision point on the calendar between now and spring, and no single document ties them together. That is the window. The readiness platform's next two phases are undated but anticipated. The appointing center's draft work statement closes for comment next week. The managed care directory rules are in the bill. The executive order's contract review falls on about January 6 and its systems milestone on about March 7, and the two record pilots will be judged against the second date whether or not a decision rule has been published. The handoff fixes in the public piece are standards, identifiers, flags, and reporting fields, which means the work underneath them is data-standard authorship, interface and identity work, measurement, and the contract modifications that carry them. A team that understands which existing vehicle each handoff rides on is reading the same calendar the government is. The platform lane sharpens that: the Army, the Air Force, and VA already hold enterprise licenses on the same engagement and case-management platform, DHA has described a care-coordination system on it, and no public document describes a shared member case between the two departments on the licenses they hold. The work underneath the public piece's "one case" is governance and configuration on signed licenses, and the January 6 contract review is where it would be written.

4. The record lane: two pilots, one decision

Confidence: High on the SAM.gov notices. Medium on scope details drawn from a J&A and an aggregator. Low on results, which are the builder's own account. Analytical on the comparison.

Four reads. First, the structure is sound: a control inside the current environment and an experiment on a separate layer is how an agency isolates what the new layer adds, and the public piece credits DHA for it. Second, the dimension that will decide the comparison is the one neither notice specifies, which is how completeness gets measured; the 2014 audit found certification was the failure point, 6040.45 already requires annual member review, and whichever pilot can show a defensible "what is missing" report at separation is answering the audit directly. Third, the reserve case is the stress test, because the documents most often missing from a reserve STR (line-of-duty determinations, remote exam results, civilian care notes) originate outside GENESIS, and a pilot scoped to GENESIS-resident data will score well on active duty and poorly on the sergeant in the public piece. Fourth, the March 7 milestone arrives before the control pilot's listed end and after the experimental pilot's estimated 180 days, so the government will be deciding with uneven evidence; a published rule for weighing it is the constructive ask, and the only one this brief makes.

The vendor-neutral design rules worth reading against any STR solution, drawn from the experimental pilot's builder and stated here as principles: every entry linked to its source document; records matched to the person with duplicates removed; anything unresolved routed to a human; every unmasking and every model call logged; models run inside the agency boundary with the application holding its own ATO; and a report of what the system could not do. [CC34] The last rule is the one the 2014 audit and the reserve records findings both point to.

Four reads. First, the handoff between the departments is no longer a procurement problem on this layer; both sides hold licenses, and VA's own outreach program is slated to move onto it. What is missing is a government-owned case model and event standard, which is a governance and configuration decision and fits the executive order's January 6 contract review. Second, the layer is an engagement layer and not a record system: the EHR, HAIMS, the readiness systems, and DEERS remain the systems of record, and a defensible design holds events and pointers, reads and writes through standard interfaces and the joint HIE, and never copies clinical content. Third, the lock-in objection is real and the answer is structural: the government owns the case model, the events, the interfaces, and the measures; every lane's contract writes to the shared case so many contractors can build different lanes; and the data contract passes a swap-out test. Fourth, the DHA row is the weak link in the public record, and the one question worth asking is whether the 2024 care-coordination system is in production and keyed to the same identifier the Army and VA use.

Three reads. First, the two 2026 fixes were a data field and a removed step, and neither required a contract, which is the pattern the public piece builds its argument on; a team working any reserve-adjacent requirement should be able to name the data field or the step its solution removes. Second, the LOD document is a record artifact with a readiness consequence on both ends: it gates care today and it gates the VA claim later, and no public STR requirement yet names it as a mandatory artifact. Third, the Georgia example is a consolidation of the exam stage at the unit level, and the remote exam program is the enterprise version of the same stage; the question for it is whether its results post to the readiness record in a form PARATUS will read, which is the exam-standard handoff in the buying map.

7. The counterargument, stated fairly

Confidence: The strongest case against the reads above, and parts of it are right.

Lanes are deliberate. Separate competitions protect small business participation, limit lock-in, and manage OCI, and DHA's EHR follow-on strategy splits work into lanes with no prime integrator on purpose. [CC46] The counter is that the argument is for owning handoffs, and a handoff standard is the thing that lets lanes stay separate without the member paying for it.

The connective layer is being built. PARATUS, the appointing center, the joint HIE, the Joint Longitudinal Viewer, and a shared EHR are all integration investments. The counter is a question: do they share a clock and an identifier, and are the contracts around them required to write to them.

Readiness is above goal. 91.1 percent beats 90. [CC47] The counter is that the number counts compliance on a date, and LOE 1 exists because lost days are invisible inside it.

Transitions hurt. T-5 moved 1.5 million beneficiaries and took most of a year to call resolved. [CC48][CC47] The counter is that a data requirement that survives the vendor change is the thing that makes the next transition cheaper.

One platform means one vendor. Enterprise licenses concentrate spend, and concentration raises cost and bargaining-power concerns. The counter is the guardrail set above: government-owned case model, events, and interfaces; a write-to-the-case clause in every lane's contract; a swap-out test. The platform can be replaced; the data contract has to survive the replacement.

The record problem is being solved. Two pilots, an industry beta, and an executive order with dates. Agreed, and the brief credits it. The remaining gap is the decision rule and whether the winning design takes in reserve, LOD, and network documents.

Capacity is the constraint. DHA projects a shortfall of "more than 8,000 clinical military medical personnel." [CC8] A handoff clock adds no clinicians. It shows where the hours go, and that is a finding a short staff can use.

8. What to read in the next ninety days

Confidence: Analytical. These are documents to pull and questions to put in writing; none is a view on how an open requirement should be written.

  1. Read the RC3 draft PWS for a readiness data element. The RFI closes November 13. The question worth asking, in writing, is whether the appointing system will receive readiness or profile status from PARATUS or the service systems, and in what form. [CC10]

  2. Watch for PARATUS AOI 2. The profiling and IMR phase is where a shared case identifier would live if DHA writes one, and its posting is the next public signal on the readiness platform. The first phase is where the exam data standard either gets written or does not. [CC4]

  3. Confirm the control pilot's period of performance on SAM.gov or FPDS. The October 2027 date is from an aggregator. If it holds, the March 7 milestone falls mid-pilot. [CC17]

  4. Mark January 6 and read what the two departments publish. The Sec. 2(b) review is the one public moment at which interoperability requirements get added to existing contracts across every lane. Which contracts are modified, and what data elements they are told to exchange, is the finding. [CC22]

  5. Read the MEND reporting product when it lands on January 31. Whether the monthly lost-warrior-day report distinguishes waiting from healing is the first test of the shared-clock argument. [CC6]

  6. Check DoD's joint SHA status. GAO had it unimplemented as of May 2025; VA's budget calls it "the new DoD One Separation Health Assessment." One call to the Military Health System's disability evaluation office, or the next GAO recommendation status update, settles it. [CC23][CC49]

  7. Pull the NDAA text when the Senate moves, for Secs. 701, 744, 761, and 776 and the Senate committee's directory and DNBI provisions. The House passed its bill in July; the Senate's stalled. Titles today; operative text decides who runs each pilot and what it must report. [CC28][CC12]

  8. Read the next JEC Joint Strategic Plan when it posts. The current plan runs out in FY2027 and is the vehicle for joint SHA, inTransition, and warm-handover commitments. [CC24]

  9. Ask the shared-case question before January 6. Whether the two departments' Sec. 2(b) contract review treats the enterprise licenses they both hold as in-scope IT contracts, and whether a shared member case model is on the list of interoperability requirements to add, is the single question that decides the platform lane. Put it in writing to both departments' review leads. [CC22][CC33]

  10. Hold the reserve artifact question. No public STR requirement names the LOD determination or the remote exam result as mandatory. Whichever pilot's exit criteria address reserve completeness first is the one that answers the sergeant. [CC30][CC13]
