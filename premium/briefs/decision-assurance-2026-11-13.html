Who Proves the Decision Was Right

How federal health agencies are redesigning evidence-heavy review and adjudication, what buyers will need to stand behind automated decisions, and whether "decision assurance" is becoming its own market.

Founding Member Brief CMS · VA · DHA · SSA Risk: the proof runs behind the automation

★ Premium Founding Member Brief

A Founding Member asked how federal health agencies are redesigning medical review, appeals, program integrity, payment review and eligibility as AI moves in, and whether a "decision assurance" market is forming. This brief answers with the three moves agencies are making, a capability map for the five things a buyer has to be able to prove, a read on where the money sits, twelve signals to watch, and the dates that matter through 2027. The question

How are federal healthcare agencies redesigning evidence-intensive review and adjudication as AI and automation advance? Assess medical review, appeals, program integrity, payment review, and eligibility. What capabilities will buyers need to ensure decisions remain accurate, measurable, traceable, defensible, and cost-effective? Is a distinct "decision assurance" market emerging, or will these capabilities remain embedded in existing contracts and technology platforms?

Asked by a Founding Member.

The short answer

The redesign is real and follows three moves. Agencies are acting earlier (before the service or the payment), letting software assemble and read the evidence while a named human signs the adverse decision, and pulling case selection back inside government.

The proof is running behind the automation. Most of the programs I reviewed cannot yet show, from public records, how often an automated step was wrong, in which direction, or whether a person or a model made the error. The strongest federal requirement applies only to systems an agency labels high-impact, and HHS gave that label to 2 of its 447 reported AI uses [1].

"Decision assurance" is not a procurement category today. I found no federal health solicitation that uses the label. The work is bought in two forms: clauses and features embedded in operating contracts and platforms, and a small set of independent validation, error-measurement, and appeals contracts.

My read: it stays mostly embedded through FY2027 and FY2028. A distinct, independent segment is most likely to grow in one place: wherever the party making the decision also has money riding on the outcome. The first buyers there are likely to be oversight bodies, not program offices.

1. How the work is being redesigned

The common thread across all five functions is a change in who does what, and when. Software now does the reading. The human role is narrowing to the adverse decision. The intervention point is moving ahead of the payment. The table shows what that looks like in each function and where the supporting proof is still thin.

FunctionWhat is changingWhere the proof is thin
Medical reviewCMS's WISeR model has technology vendors review prior authorization requests in six states, with a clinician required before any non-affirmation [2, 3]. The vendor is paid 25% of averted spending, adjusted by a quality score, and loses the payment if the denial is overturned on appeal [2]. On the measurement side, the new CERT (Comprehensive Error Rate Testing) review contractor (Serco, $109M over seven years) pairs independent medical reviewers with AI document processing [4].As of mid-August CMS had released no WISeR denial, appeal, or overturn rates [5]. Letters released October 27 through a public-records lawsuit show CMS confronting four of six vendors in the first quarter over backlogs, missing decision letters, or denial patterns it questioned [6].
AppealsI found no public evidence that Medicare appeals bodies use AI to decide cases, and the February 2026 privacy assessment for the Medicare hearings case system answers no to the AI question [7]. What has changed is the job appeals are being asked to do: they are becoming the accuracy check on automated first decisions. CMS has proposed adding "approved after appeal" counts to payers' public prior authorization metrics [8]. SSA plans AI to support drafting dispositions, and its hearings adjudicators already use a tool that flags quality problems in draft decisions [9, 10]. CMS issued a QIC program RFI on October 15 and moved the Part C independent review work to C2C effective May 1 [11, 12].Overturn rates only measure the cases that get appealed. HHS OIG found Medicare Advantage plans overturned 95% of appealed nursing-facility denials, but only 18% of denials were appealed [13]. At VA, higher-level reviewers returned about 35% of decisions in FY2026, up from 23% in FY2021 [14].
Program integrityCMS runs more than 300 models that watch provider billing and can suspend payment during an investigation [15]. Its October 5 RFI proposes that CMS, not contractors, drive lead generation and prioritization. Contractors would build the cases, and would need CMS approval to use AI or other automation [16, 17].GAO found CMS does not track the primary source of leads or whether prepayment denials are tied to its Fraud Prevention System [18]. Public statements give savings totals, not model accuracy or false-positive rates [15].
Payment reviewTreasury screened 1.1 billion payments worth about $3.7 trillion before disbursement in FY2026 [19]. DHA is setting up Do Not Pay matching for the TRICARE Overseas Program [20]. VA's PIVOT RFI seeks a SaaS tool that scores community care claims before payment [21]. CMS has HealthEdge and Peraton competing to replace its legacy claims system with real-time payment and fraud review [22].TRICARE's improper payment measurement still does not examine the underlying medical record, an open GAO priority recommendation [23]. An outside commentary reports that DHA pilot record reviews found documentation error rates of 28% and 67% in the two regions, against a reported rate under 1% [24]. Governmentwide improper payments rose to about $186 billion in FY2025; Medicare fee-for-service was 6.55% [25, 26].
EligibilityMedicaid work requirements start by January 1, 2027, with CMS routing verification data through its Federal Data Services Hub [27, 28]. VA plans to apply Automated Decision Support to 90% of disability claims by the end of 2027 [14]. SSA set a 93% target for electronically acquired medical evidence in FY2026, up from 60% actual in FY2025; the FY2026 measure also counts faxed evidence routed to the electronic folder [9].There is no good data source for medical frailty, likely the largest work-requirement exemption [28]. VA OIG found nearly all sampled automated death-benefit decisions or letters had a legal or procedural deficiency [29]. VA does not separate automated from human errors in its statistics [14]. GAO found CMS does not analyze state Medicaid eligibility errors and fixes across states and years [30]. The SSA inspector general says SSA still needs to evaluate whether its automation is working as intended [31].

Three things stand out

  • The human is being kept on the denial, not on the file. WISeR requires clinician review before a non-affirmation, Medicare Advantage rules require a health professional on medical-necessity denials, and VA says a claims processor reviews every claim before it is final [2, 32, 33]. That protects the adverse decision. It does not, by itself, tell anyone whether the approvals were right or whether the evidence summary the human relied on was complete.
  • Government is taking back the judgment calls and leaving contractors the labor. The CMS fraud RFI is the clearest case: CMS picks the targets, contractors do claims analysis, medical review, and evidence collection. CMS also asked how to price that work so it does not reward the volume of investigations or referrals over their quality [17].
  • Incentive design is doing the work that measurement should be doing. WISeR relies on a quality multiplier and appeal recoupment to keep a vendor paid on averted spending honest [2]. CMS says it will audit vendors against coverage criteria, feed the results into quality scores, and may remove a vendor with a high inaccuracy rate [34]. I found no published audit method, sample size, or result, and no separate validation contract. The older recovery audit program took a different path: a separate validation contractor and a first-level appeal overturn limit below 10% [35, 36].

2. What buyers will need

OMB already sets the floor. Its April 2025 guidance treats AI as high-impact when its output is a principal basis for decisions on benefits, public insurance care allocation, continued eligibility, or fraud detection, and requires testing before deployment, an impact assessment, ongoing monitoring, human oversight, and a working remedy or appeal path [37]. The companion acquisition memo requires contracts to let the agency evaluate performance on a regular schedule using agency-held data the vendor cannot see [38]. The five attributes in the question map onto requirements as follows. The middle column is my analysis; the right column shows where each requirement has already appeared.

AttributeCapability the buyer needsWhere it already shows up
AccurateTest cases the government owns and the vendor has not seen. Error measured in both directions: wrongful denials and wrongful approvals. Results split by case difficulty, so easy cases do not flatter the tool.OMB M-25-22 independent evaluation terms [38]. VA describes independent logic-accuracy checks before deployment and reports 97.8% extraction accuracy for AI evidence gathering [33]. VA's comparison of automated and traditional claim quality has no published method that matches case complexity [39].
MeasurableA tag on every decision showing which steps were automated. Overturn rates reported next to appeal rates. Timeliness that counts extensions and re-submissions.CMS-0062-P would add counts for extended, denied, and approved-after-appeal requests [8]. The gap is visible at VA and CMS, where automated and human errors or model-linked denials are not tracked separately [14, 18].
TraceableA per-decision record: the evidence used, the rule and model version, the reviewer, and every change. A flag when a record in the file was itself AI-generated.VA's PIVOT RFI asks for audit trails, rule version control and rollback, explainable outputs with confidence scores, and claim-to-payment traceability [21]. SSA workshop speakers called for provenance and metadata on AI-touched records [40].
DefensibleA named, qualified person on each adverse decision. Notices that state the reasons and the favorable findings. Records that hold up in an appeal, an inspector general review, or a public-records request.OMB M-25-21 oversight and remedy practices [37]. VA OIG estimated at least 7,000 automated notices omitted favorable findings [29]. WISeR vendor letters became public through litigation [6]. GAO warned that opaque AI can limit error detection and complicate veterans' appeals [41].
Cost-effectivePrice tied to correct decisions, not to denials, referrals, or recoveries. Downstream appeal and rework cost counted. Portability and transparent pricing so the agency can switch.CMS asked industry for pricing that avoids rewarding volume [17]. M-25-22 lock-in and data-rights terms [38]. GAO lists AI pricing and continuous evaluation among recurring acquisition challenges [42].

The floor covers fewer systems than it appears to

OMB's requirements attach only when an agency designates a use as high-impact, and agencies are applying that label very differently. In the FY2025 inventories, VA reported 215 of the 445 high-impact uses governmentwide [43]. HHS reported 447 uses and designated two as high-impact, one of them a CMS identity verification tool used in Medicaid eligibility. Another 13 were presumed high-impact and then reclassified, in at least one case because a human verifies the output [1]. SSA lists nine high-impact uses, including its medical-record reading tool and its models that select claims for quality review, each marked as independently reviewed by the agency's chief AI officer or another internal office [10].

Two consequences follow. First, keeping a human on the decision can serve both as a safeguard and as a reason not to apply the stricter rules. Second, where agencies do apply the rules, the independent review is mostly being done inside government. VA describes its own as a two-party review by its chief AI officer team and does not say whether results are published [44].

One practical point for buyers: the government already holds the answer key. Years of adjudicated claims, appeal outcomes, and error-rate samples are a ready test set. A Stanford brief published this week makes the same argument and recommends third-party evaluation as a condition of procurement [45]. The agencies that organize those records first will be able to write measurable accuracy terms. The rest will fall back on vendor attestations, which is what VA's contract templates use today [33].

3. Is a distinct market emerging?

Not as a named category. The spending exists, but it sits in three places, and most of it is inside something else.

Where it sitsExamplesWhat it tells us
Independent line itemsRecovery Auditor Validation Contract: J29, $17.2M over five years, total small business set-aside [35]. CERT review contractor: Serco, $109M over seven years [4]. Independent appeals: Part C review entity to C2C; QIC program RFI [12, 11].Small next to the operating contracts they check, but structurally protected. Each exists because the checker cannot be the operator.
Embedded in operating contractsWISeR quality multiplier and appeal recoupment [2]. VA work statements with contractor attestations to OMB AI requirements [33]. Proposed CMS fraud contracts with quality-based pricing [17].This is where most of the requirement lands. The operator produces the proof as a deliverable.
Embedded in platformsVA PIVOT requires explainability, audit logging, and rule versioning from the tool itself [21]. VA's CuraClaims automates intake through audit with a human confirming [46]. Commercial vendors now market a claim-level decision trace [47].The trace is becoming a product feature. Recovery audit work is also consolidating: Performant now operates as a Machinify company [48].
Inside governmentVA's Automation Quality Program and a pre-deployment "Claims Processing Automation Quality Auditor" use case [33, 49]. HHS OIG audit of HHS AI governance, announced July 2 [50].Agencies are building some of this themselves, which limits what gets contracted out.

How large the lines are

The operating side is an order of magnitude larger than the checking side. The current CMS fraud-investigation vehicle has a $2.5 billion ceiling and about $1.83 billion obligated since 2016, with ordering through May 9, 2027 [51]. A trade analysis counts three firms holding five regional task orders, all cost-plus-award-fee [52]. CMS forecasts a new Supplemental Medical Review Contract at $100 million or more, with a solicitation planned for December 15, 2026 and award in May 2027, plus several Medicare Advantage audit lines in the $20 million to $50 million range as it expands those audits from 60 plan contracts to about 590 per payment year [53]. By contrast, the two independent measurement and validation awards I could price come to roughly $19 million a year combined [4, 35]. In my review of the November forecast I did not find a line labeled as validation or assurance of automated decisions [53]. One commercial research firm puts government agencies at about $2.5 billion of an $18.3 billion global payment integrity market in 2026. That figure is a vendor estimate whose method I have not reviewed, and it measures payment integrity work, not assurance [54].

Why it stays mostly embedded

  • OMB's rules attach the obligation to the AI use case and its contract, so the default path is a clause in the operating award, not a separate buy [37, 38].
  • CMS is moving judgment in-house, and its budget justification says its contract review is reducing overall contract spending [17, 55]. That is not an environment for new stand-alone categories.
  • The one attempt to build a stand-alone, third-party testing layer for health AI did not hold. The Coalition for Health AI dropped its national assurance lab network in February 2026 after HHS leadership opposed it, and moved toward helping health systems monitor tools after deployment [56]. That is a commercial precedent, not a federal procurement, but it shows the current posture toward outside certifiers.
  • Platform vendors are bundling the decision record into the system that makes the decision, and the largest pending buy, the Medicare claims system replacement, includes real-time fraud review in the core platform [22].

Where a distinct segment can grow

The exception is independence. Where the decision-maker is paid from the result, the buyer eventually needs someone else to check the work. Medicare has done this before with the recovery audit validation contractor. WISeR vendors are paid a share of averted spending. CMS says it will audit them, but I found no separate validator and no published results [2, 34]. Medicaid eligibility vendors, according to an interim report by Senate Finance Committee Democrats, did not provide meaningful detail on accuracy testing [57]. Those are the two most likely openings.

The earliest buyer may not be the program office. On October 16 the Ohio Auditor of State sought a vendor to test the accuracy of eligibility determinations made through the state benefits system, with one scenario covering 487,427 determinations and work due April 1, 2027 [58]. That is decision assurance in everything but name, bought by an oversight body. On the plan side, a House bill introduced in July would require Medicare Advantage plans to keep a signed physician attestation and ten years of records for each AI-based denial, report every 90 days, and submit to audits that compare AI outputs with denials [59]. It has only been introduced. Expect the federal openings to arrive as task orders on existing program integrity, quality, or verification-and-validation vehicles rather than under a new name.

The strongest case against my read. A separate market could form faster than I expect. The conditions are in place: OMB already requires independent evaluation using data the vendor cannot access; Congress has pressed on WISeR, with a House committee amendment to block FY2027 funding and a 46 to 50 Senate vote that fell short of ending it; inspectors general at VA and HHS are auditing automated decisions and AI governance; at least one federal court has allowed discovery into an insurer's use of AI in claim denials; and a federal court in Idaho held that an unreliable Medicaid budget tool violated due process [38, 5, 29, 50, 60, 61]. A state auditor is already buying independent testing of eligibility decisions, and a pending House bill would write audit rights over AI denials into Medicare Advantage [58, 59]. If one high-profile program is ordered to add an outside validator, other programs may copy the structure quickly. What I do not see yet is the funding line or the solicitation. Until one appears, this remains a requirement set, not a market.

6. What this means for industry

  • If you operate the decision (review contractor, administrator, eligibility vendor): plan to deliver the decision record as a contract deliverable, and expect pricing tied to denials or recoveries to draw scrutiny. The CMS fraud RFI and the WISeR record both point that way [17, 6].
  • If you are independent of operations: the validation, error-measurement, and appeals lines are small but durable, and one of them was a total small business set-aside [35]. The opening is to bring measurement methods for AI-assisted work to those existing lines before the recompetes, not to wait for a new category.
  • If you sell the platform: explainable outputs, audit logging, and rule versioning are already written into a VA requirement [21]. The differentiator will be letting the agency test the system with its own data and take the record with it [38].
  • Know who signs the check. The buyers most likely to pay for independent testing first are auditors, inspectors general, and error-measurement offices. Program offices are more likely to ask the operator for the evidence [58, 4].
  • For everyone: describe the capability in the buyer's terms. Agencies are not asking for "decision assurance." They are asking for accuracy they can verify, appeals that hold, and prices that do not reward volume.

Method and limits

This brief draws on public agency documents, GAO and inspector general reports, solicitation notices, and trade and general press published through November 13, 2026, plus Mission Meets Tech reporting. Trade and press sources are named as such in the source list. I did not have the draft Statement of Objectives attached to the CMS fraud RFI and relied on the notice, a law firm summary, and a trade analysis. The WISeR measure definitions sit in a separate CMS reporting guide I did not review. The HHS high-impact count comes from a think tank analysis of the FY2025 inventory, the most recent available; the FY2026 inventory may differ. Contract values from HigherGov, GovConIC, and OrangeSlices are trade compilations of public award data. I did not query the Mission Meets Tech premium database for this brief. Public evidence on TRICARE and on AI inside appeals bodies is thinner than for CMS and VA, and absence of public evidence is not proof of absence. A 2020 CMS sources-sought notice shows the agency has considered AI payment recommendations "without human review of the medical record" before; I treat it as background, not a current plan [63]. The three-move framework, the capability map, the market read, and the signal list are my analysis.
