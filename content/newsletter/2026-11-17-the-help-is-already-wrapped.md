# The Help Is Already Wrapped

*Military and veteran families wrote to Globe Santa this year. Most of the help they asked for already exists, and the fastest way it reaches a family this December is a person who cares enough to make the introduction. This issue is a letter, a short list, and one ask.*

Friends,

Picture Sarah. She is an Air Force spouse, and her husband is deployed. Their toddler is running a fever, she has barely slept, and the bills are stacking up on the kitchen counter. It is the second week of December. There is a small tree in the corner, and after the house goes quiet she sits beside it and works out what can go under it once the rent clears. She will not say a word about any of this on the phone to her husband, because he has enough to carry.

This month The Boston Globe printed letters from families like hers. Every year parents around Boston write to Globe Santa, a 71-year-old program of the Boston Globe Foundation that brings holiday gifts to children in need, and this year Linda Matchan found military and veteran families in the stack. One request came from a four-year-old whose father is a disabled veteran. Another came from a mother whose Navy husband is in Japan, in a family that has gone more than a year without being in the same room. [1] Those families deserve to be heard in full. Please read Matchan's piece here: https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/11/08/metro/military-families-face-staggering-economic-pressures/

Mandy Bloomingdale has been where Sarah is. She is a military spouse near Fort Campbell, and more than a decade ago her husband went two and a half months without a paycheck. Operation Homefront showed up for her family that year, and she says that without it, "my children would not have had a Christmas." She told that story last December from the giving side of the table, at Operation Homefront's holiday event in Oak Grove, Kentucky, where she was helping hand gifts and holiday meal cards to 275 families. [2]

Somebody helped Mandy's family through a hard December. She has been handing it forward since 2012.

## Why I am writing this one

I write about contracts, budgets, and software most weeks, and I love that work. This week I am writing about kitchens.

These are our families. They pack the house, change schools, and start over in a new town, and then they do it again two or three years later so that someone they love can do a hard job for the rest of us. [1] When one of them has to ask a newspaper for help with Christmas, I want every person who reads this newsletter to know exactly where to send them.

## What Shannon heard

Matchan interviewed Shannon Razsadin, who runs the Military Family Advisory Network. Shannon told her that most Americans assume military families have the basics covered, when many are "struggling to make ends meet." [1] Afterward Shannon wrote that her team spends its days on data and strategy, and that the conversation reminded her "behind every data point is a person." [3] On November 5, with support from USAA, MFAN released a national profile of currently serving military families, and three people named Cyndee, Ari, and Anna shared their own experiences of military life with everyone in the room. [3][4] That takes courage, and I am grateful to all three.

MFAN's numbers are painful. In its 2025 survey of more than 10,000 military and veteran family members, about four in ten were food insecure by the Department of Agriculture's six-question measure, and about a third of currently serving families had less than $500 put away for an emergency, or nothing. [5] The survey was taken during a government shutdown, and MFAN says plainly that the timing may have shaped the answers. [5] Even so, these are our neighbors, and I believe them.

## The part that gives me hope

The same survey found that about six in ten active-duty parents did not know about the military's child care subsidy, and seven in ten active-duty spouses had gone two years without using a formal employment resource. [5] There is hope in that. The help has been built. The family and the help have not been introduced.

Pride is part of it. Vivian Dietrich of Operation Homefront told Federal News Network last November that families tend to call at the last hour because "the military is very proud." [6] It is a good kind of pride, and it means the introduction has to come from someone the family already trusts: a first sergeant, a supervisor, a clinician, a neighbor, you.

There is a question I built this newsletter on: does this save lives or enhance readiness? The Department of War answers this one in its own guide for military leaders: "A military family's economic security is critical to mission readiness." [7] A sailor stands an easier watch when the house back home is warm and the kids have a Christmas.

And these families still believe in the work. Six in ten told MFAN they would recommend a military career, a share that has risen since 2023, and more than three in four currently serving families said the mission itself met or beat what they expected. [5] They are holding up their end. It is our turn.

Everything below already exists. It runs on a gift card, a Saturday, a phone number, and one person willing to say a name out loud before December 15.

## Adopt a family

Soldiers' Angels has military and veteran families signed up and waiting right now, because their registration closed November 10. You get a real family and a real wish list. You buy a gift for each child, about $50 to $75 apiece, add a grocery card of $75 to $125 for the holiday meal, and get it there by December 15. One person can take up to three families, a company can take as many as it can carry, and last year adopters reached 1,940 families and 5,547 children. A donation covers a family still waiting when time runs out. [8] If your company gives at year end, one staff meeting can decide this. Adopting and donating both start on the same page: https://soldiersangels.org/adopt-a-family

There are other good places for a gift. Operation Homefront has put more than 210,000 holiday meals on military and veteran tables since 2010. At one of its events in Houston, a hundred families got dinner, pictures with Santa, and a surprise visit from an astronaut, and a Marine veteran named Scotti Fraser said that without it his family's holiday meal would have been "leftovers and sandwiches." This year's events run into the first week of December, from Lawton, Oklahoma, to Newport News, Virginia. [9] Give here: https://operationhomefront.org/donate/

Globe Santa reached nearly 27,000 children last year, and it is where the four-year-old's letter went. [1][10] Your gift can go to the same place: https://www.globesanta.org/

Our Military Kids will put a child whose Guard or Reserve parent is deployed, or whose parent is recovering from combat injuries, into a sport or an art class for $300. [11] Fund one here: https://ourmilitarykids.org/donate

MFAN meets families who have just moved to a new installation with a box of pantry staples and, for those who qualify, $125 toward groceries. [12] MFAN's giving page: https://www.mfan.org/topic/get-involved/ways-to-give/

## Show up

Every one of these organizations runs on volunteers in December, and Mandy is one of them. The Armed Services YMCA's branches hold Operation Holiday Joy events all season, and they need hands to sort, pack, and pass out meals and toys. Julianna Burson, a military spouse who writes for the organization, remembers walking into a chapel in San Diego that volunteers had turned into a toy workshop, in a year when her family was getting through a hard return from deployment. [13] A team from your office can be those volunteers on one Saturday morning. You will meet the families your work is for, and you will remember it.

The Armed Services YMCA lists volunteer openings by branch: https://asymca.org/how-to-help/volunteer

Operation Homefront, where Mandy volunteers, signs people up here: https://operationhomefront.org/volunteer/

## Say the name out loud

This one costs nothing, and for some families it is the only thing that works. At the Armed Services YMCA in Hawaii last year, a family could join Operation Holiday Joy only if a command, a chaplain, or a family support agency referred them, and the referrals were due November 7. [13] Operation Ride Home, which has sent 14,361 junior enlisted service members, spouses, and children home for the holidays since 2011, took this year's Hawaii applications from September 4 to October 9. [14] Some of the best holiday help opens in September and closes before the rest of us have bought a turkey. A leader who knows that can put it on the calendar in August and tell the squad. Find the branch nearest you and ask how its referrals work: https://asymca.org/locations/

If you lead people in uniform or in a government office, the Department has already written your script. The Military Leaders Economic Security Toolkit on Military OneSource shows you how to spot food insecurity and where to send someone next, and Military OneSource picks up around the clock at 800-342-9647. [7] If your company employs veterans or Guard and Reserve members, the same script works in your building. The toolkit is here: https://www.militaryonesource.mil/leaders-service-providers/economic-security/

And if the person in front of you is a veteran having a hard December, please keep one more number in your phone. The Veterans Crisis Line is 988, then press 1. It is there for veterans, service members, and the people who love them, whether or not the veteran is enrolled in VA. They can also text 838255. [15] The online chat is here: https://www.veteranscrisisline.net/get-help-now/chat/

## The load at home

I started this newsletter in March of last year, and the second issue was called "When Tech Lifts the Load." It argued that technology could give military clinicians back the hours that paperwork takes. [16] This issue is about a different load, the one Sarah carries beside that tree after the house goes quiet. You can lift some of it with a gift card, a Saturday, or a phone number.

Please pick one of the three before December 15. Then write back and tell me who is doing this well where you live, because I would love for the list to be longer than mine.

To Shannon, to Mandy, to Kara Dallman and her team at Our Military Kids, and to everyone who will spend a December Saturday handing a present to a child they have never met: thank you. You make it easy to be proud of this community.

A four-year-old wrote to Globe Santa this fall. Her father served. Somebody is going to answer her, and it could be one of us.

Let's roll.

— Mary

Mission Meets Tech
