# The Pencil Test

*A hospital executive in Las Vegas last week said he expected someone to hold up a pencil and call it AI-enabled. The software that writes the clinic note now sells itself for the order, the insurance form, the phone call, and the bill. VA, the Defense Health Agency, and HHS each have to decide which of those jobs it gets next, and a pencil turns out to be a hard act to follow.*

Friends,

Dr. Darryl Elmouchi is the chief operating officer of Providence, and last week at HLTH, the health technology conference in Las Vegas, he said he "expected someone to hold up a pencil and say it's AI-enabled." [1] A survey released at the same conference scored 72 health startups, found that 72 percent of them open their pitch by calling the product AI-powered, and reported that none of the investors it surveyed said the phrase raised their confidence. [2]

Until about a year ago, a pencil was a fair description of the most successful AI product in medicine. The AI scribe listened to a visit and wrote the note. A clinician read the note and signed it, and nothing else in the hospital moved because of it. This year the same software began placing orders, proposing billing codes, filling in insurance forms, and answering the phone.

A pencil, for the record, is in every drawer, has a few centuries of evidence behind it, and stops when the hand does. Very little of what was sold in Las Vegas can claim all three.

Take a knee that has ached for a month. A clinician writes the note and orders an MRI. A staff member asks the insurer for permission, another calls the patient with a time, a coder sends the bill, and one day the patient asks for a copy of the record. That is six pieces of work from one visit, and this fall there is software on sale for every one of them.

## The note

The note is the job the software has held longest, and the market for it has settled into something ordinary. A report from Menlo Ventures, released the week of the conference, sizes the scribe market at $950 million and says incumbents captured 68 percent of the new money this year, with Microsoft and Epic taking "roughly two of every three new scribe dollars." Every major record vendor now sells a scribe of its own. [3] A product that comes bundled with the record system is a feature, and I expect it to be bought like one.

The evidence for the feature is real and modest. The randomized trials were published in late 2025. In one, a scribe cut the time clinicians spent in notes by 9.5 percent, while a second product in the same trial showed no significant change. An editorial in the same journal put the best case at about 22 minutes a day and called the tools "not productivity tools (yet)." [4] The University of Wisconsin reports that its trial returned about 30 minutes a day and lowered burnout. [5] All of that work tested the product versions of 2024 and 2025, so the best evidence available is already a step behind what is on sale.

The Defense Health Agency tried its tool with about 400 providers at four sites in late 2025, at Madigan, Camp Lejeune, Portsmouth, and Wilford Hall, and phased it into military hospitals and clinics worldwide this year. [6] Lt. Col. Matthew Royall, a pediatric gastroenterologist who is also the agency's deputy branch chief of ambulatory informatics, has described how a visit works with it: the provider asks the patient's consent, records the encounter, and then reviews and signs the note the software drafted. [6] By July, Dr. Jesus Caban of DHA was counting more than 30,000 providers and 500,000 notes. [7] The tool is Oracle's Clinical AI Agent, from the vendor behind the military's health record. [6][8]

VA took a different road. It set up a five-year enterprise contract for ambient scribes with a ceiling of $775.72 million, a figure that caps what all the vendors on the contract can be paid together and says nothing about what has been spent. [9] Abridge says its product reaches VA medical centers through a distribution partner, and Knowtex, another company on the contract, says the scope extends past the note into coding and order entry. [9][10]

Community health centers, the clinics HHS supports through its health center program, got their offer at the conference itself. Abridge announced that its assistant will be free to the 108,000 clinicians who work in them. [11] The same week Kyu Rhee, who leads the National Association of Community Health Centers, warned that Medicaid cuts could force as many as 1,800 of those centers to close. [1] Free software and a closure warning reached the same clinics in the same week.

By Royall's description, nothing enters the record until a clinician has read it and signed. The hand is still on the pencil. The note is also the smallest thing the software can make from a visit it has just heard.

## The order

In February, Oracle added order creation to its Clinical AI Agent. In August it added billing codes, dictation, and chart review, and in September a version for nurses. [12] Epic's answer, a product called Ergo Visit, prepares a summary before the appointment and then assembles the documentation, the orders, and the diagnoses while it listens, and as of August it was live at three organizations. [13] Abridge's platform, announced in June, generates orders and billing codes and adds decision support. [14] On November 17 Ambience launched software that follows inpatient rounds and tracks whether a patient is ready to go home, and its announcement names no controls for human approval. [15]

The proof here is thinner than it was for the note. Most of what is known about these features comes from the companies selling them, the Epic sites are few, and Oracle's own roadmap still lists prior authorization and charge capture as work ahead. [12]

The distance between a note and an order is the distance between a record and an act. A wrong sentence in a note waits in the chart until somebody corrects it. A wrong order travels to a pharmacy, a lab, or a scanner. At the conference Mark Kaplan of HCA described the price of a single visible error: once a physician sees an inaccuracy, he said, it is "really hard to regain their trust." [16]

In a report this year, VA's inspector general found that VA had no safety-event reporting designed for AI-written notes and recommended building AI risk monitoring into its patient safety programs, and VA agreed. [17] That finding concerned the note, and the order raises the stakes.

Oracle's leaders spent two days on Capitol Hill last week explaining how VA's contract for its record system, the same one military hospitals run, grew from a $10 billion ceiling to $27 billion. [18] The AI came up in passing. Mike Sicilia, the company's co-CEO, told senators that the price VA pays includes access to clinical AI, and he told the House committee that Oracle wants VA using its clinical AI agent. [18][19] On the military side the tool has a budget line: the Defense Health Program's fiscal 2027 request includes a $200.4 million digital health increase that covers ambient listening implementation along with expanded video visits and tele-critical care. [20] Seema Verma, who runs Oracle's health business, answered a question about data terms by saying the company has "zero access to the data of the veterans." [19]

At DHA the next decisions will come as product updates, and a feature that arrives inside an upgrade is the easiest kind to switch on without deciding to. My read is that each feature Oracle ships is a separate choice for DHA's Medical Digital Solutions portfolio, to be made with its own test and its own safety review, and that a written yes or no for each one is the cheapest safeguard on offer.

CMS is approaching the same line as a regulator. Dr. Mehmet Oz, who runs the agency, told the conference by video that state testing programs are a way to find the point where software stops assisting a clinician and starts making a decision that takes a license. [21]

An order seldom travels alone. Before that knee gets its MRI, somebody has to ask the insurer.

## The form

The asking happens in portals, the insurer websites where staff retype what the chart already says. At the conference OpenAI announced agents that read a screen and click through it the way a person would, built, in the company's words, for "steps that APIs do not fully support." [22] CVS Health has them in production, where by the companies' account one backlog was cleared over a single weekend. Cigna is using them across more than ten systems, Abridge is building with them, and the prior authorization workflow that Ambience showed was a demonstration. [22][23] A separate pilot with a company called Tennr will compare agents against staff on prior authorization and has no results yet. [24]

OpenAI's evidence is its own. In the company's evaluation, reviewers rated 99 percent of 325 record-system tasks as clinically safe, and its results on administrative tasks went unpublished. [23] The brake is better documented than the proof. Every workflow the company described keeps a person in control: the organization decides which steps need human review, each action is logged, and Karan Singhal, who leads OpenAI's health AI work, said the models "might overreach" without sign-off. [22][23]

Federal health is a natural customer for an unflattering reason: an agent that works by reading screens suits the systems with the fewest modern connections. I suspect VA's older record system and the paperwork of community care hold plenty of those screens, and that TRICARE referrals hold their share. In such places the audit log carries extra weight, because it may be the only account of what the agent did. Epic said outside agents will be able to pull data from its records through a standard connection by the first quarter of 2027, with a developer marketplace to follow in March. [25]

While the form is pending, the patient with the knee is waiting for a phone call.

## The phone call

Software can now follow a physician on inpatient rounds and track which patients are ready to go home. [15] This summer VA's inspector general reported what happened when veterans called the clinics it sampled: of about 2.9 million calls, roughly 573,000 went to wrong numbers and about 530,000 went to voicemail. [26]

At the conference Amazon made its phone agents for health systems generally available. They book appointments, handle prescription requests, reset portal passwords, and answer questions, tasks Amazon says cover up to 60 percent of inbound calls. At UC San Diego, the company reports, the agent that verifies a caller's identity cut staff phone time by 630 hours a week and abandoned calls by about 30 percent. [27] Those are a vendor's figures about its own product.

DHA has described its own version of the problem in a contracting notice, where it called appointing "fragmented across over 110 separate contracts and call centers." [28] I expect the same arithmetic wherever a federal health program picks up the phone to check eligibility or book a slot, Medicare's lines at CMS included.
After the scan and the call comes the bill, and the bill is where this technology has begun to pay its own way.

## The bill

Medicare still runs claims through a system written in COBOL in 1979. Oz used his remarks to say CMS will move it to the cloud at a cost of about $1 billion, with operating costs projected to fall at least 5 percent against a baseline he did not give, and he asked technologists to come work at CMS. [21] He paired all of that with a proposed rule, called CRUSH, that would require Medicare Advantage and Part D plans to refer suspected fraud, something only 46 percent of plan contracts did voluntarily in 2025. CMS projects about $90 billion in savings over ten years, and comments are due January 19, 2027. [21]

So the country's largest payer is rebuilding the machinery that reads claims in the same season that the companies writing clinical notes began selling on what those notes are worth. Ambience says one customer needed a 1.5 percent rise in its case-mix index, a measure of patient complexity, to justify the purchase and reported 6.5 percent. [15] Suki, another scribe company, now offers to refund up to 100 percent of its fees when agreed targets are missed, and those targets include coding accuracy and risk-adjustment capture. [29]

The evidence on coding is suggestive and contested. A Trilliant Health study of six systems found that the share of new-patient visits billed at the highest levels rose by 12 to 20 points after scribes arrived, and because the study had no control group, it shows an association. [30] The Wisconsin trial found that billing compliance held, and a fuller note can honestly support a higher code. [5] Payers have reached their own conclusion: in the Menlo survey, 43 percent of them, and 57 percent of the large ones, said provider AI coding raised their cost trend for 2026. [3]

VA and TRICARE are payers too. VA paid $44.8 billion for community care in fiscal 2026, $31.2 billion of it to hospitals and physicians, and a report posted to health.mil on November 18 shows TRICARE's purchased care finishing the year $629.9 million short. [31][32] The providers those programs pay are the providers buying these tools, and a payer that tracks the share of high-level visits in its claims will know whether anything has moved. Neither figure has been tied to AI coding, and the TRICARE report does not attribute its shortfall to it. That tracking would fall to VA's Office of Integrated Veteran Care and DHA's TRICARE Health Plan.

Inside federal hospitals the sales pitch changes. A VA medical center or a military hospital is funded by appropriation, with billing a small part of its budget, so the value of a scribe has to be counted in clinician time, retention, data quality, and readiness. That is the case DHA makes for its own tool. [6]

Then one day the patient with the knee asks for a copy of all of it.

## The record

Fifteen days. That is how long a provider will have to answer a patient's request for records under the final HIPAA access rule that Paula Stannard, who directs the HHS Office for Civil Rights, walked through in Las Vegas, and the limit used to be 30. The rule also requires providers to send records to a qualifying "personal health application" that the patient controls, bars unreasonable identity checks, and requires fees to be posted online. HHS estimates $784 million in first-year costs and $703 million a year in net savings over the four years that follow. [33] Amy Gleason of CMS promoted the agency's voluntary Health Tech Ecosystem, an industry pledge that the conference's session page says more than 600 organizations have backed, and her advice to the room was to "stop waiting on regulation." [2][34]

In March the national coordinator for health IT at HHS, Dr. Thomas Keen, told a Senate hearing that once patients control their information, "they have freedom to do with it what they want." [35] The protections that cover a record inside a hospital stay behind when it moves into an app. Eight months later the department has written a rule that sends the record to the app on a fifteen-day clock. What a qualifying app owes the patient after the record arrives is the part of the rule to read first.

My read is that the rule reaches federal care directly, since VA medical centers and military hospitals are covered entities under HIPAA and answer the same requests. How the fifteen-day clock fits with the departments' own record-release policies is still an open question. The test case is a service member in the last month of a career, who will need that record for every claim that follows.

The pencil in the joke had one more part.

## The eraser

A pencil moves only when a hand moves it. It leaves a mark anyone can read afterward, and it comes with an eraser. The industry spent its week in Las Vegas selling all three under other names: human review, audit logs, permission limits. It added a fourth, shadow mode, in which an agent shows what it would do before it may act. A panel moderated by Amy Truong of the CMS Innovation Center, with Epic, NVIDIA, and HCA on stage, settled on accountability first and autonomy second. The panel agreed that plain rules-based software remains the cheapest and safest option, and Epic's speaker said it is staying. [16]

The company now offering agents that click through hospital screens knows why. In August OpenAI reported that models had "circumvented controls designed to isolate them from the internet." [36] Its health announcement arrives with lists of permitted actions, approval gates, and a log of every step.

Buyers have added a brake of their own in the contract. Health systems are signing agreements of a year or less tied to clear benchmarks, a few of them as short as three months. [1] VA's contract runs five years. The task orders under it can still carry benchmarks of the same kind, and the measures I would write in are time in notes, after-hours time in the record, error rates in notes, and the mix of visit levels. DHA's campaign plan sets a target of 67 percent adoption of ambient documentation by May 30, 2027, with an engagement strategy due December 31. [37] Adoption counts how many clinicians use the tool, and the commercial market has started pricing what the tool returns.

The last brake is the meter. Google Cloud told the conference that each unit of AI processing keeps getting cheaper while total use is "exploding," and the insurer Elevance said it expects costs to rise in every market in the short term. [38] About 40 percent of health executives in a Google Cloud survey said they had paused AI projects over unpredictable costs. [39]

## What the skeptics have right

The strongest objection is that much of this is announcement. Ergo Visit is at a handful of sites, the Ambience prior authorization workflow was a demonstration, Oracle's is still on a roadmap, the Tennr pilot has no results, and by one trade account much of Suki's agent work is still planned. [12][13][24][29] A year from now there will be results to argue about.

The coding worry may be wrong on the merits. Higher codes can be more accurate codes, and the study that started the argument had no control group. [30] Asking for proof can be unfair to anything new, because trials take years and report on old versions, and a recent review of the scribe literature found analyses of cost and return "largely absent." [40] Federal health may also decide to wait for its record vendors, which would leave little for anyone else to sell.

All of that is fair. It also costs nothing to write down, before a feature is switched on, whether anyone is using it, what the proof is, and who can stop it. A pencil answers by being a pencil. Software has to be asked.

## The hour

Dr. John Whyte, the chief executive of the American Medical Association, brought a worry to the conference: that the time these tools return will be claimed by the schedule and filled with more patients, when it could go to longer visits or better decisions. [1]

In a commercial health system that tension gets settled by revenue. In a VA medical center or a military hospital it gets settled by a decision, because the returned hour is nearly the whole return. Someone has to say what the hour is for.

Which job the software gets next is a federal decision with names on it. VA's open item is the inspector general's recommendation on safety monitoring, which sits with the Under Secretary for Health. [17] DHA owes a yes or no on each feature that arrives in an update, and HHS has put two of its choices on paper, a proposed rule on fraud whose comment period closes January 19 and a final rule on records. [21][33] What the returned time is for belongs in the same document as the adoption target.

Royall, the pediatric gastroenterologist at DHA, has already given one answer. "Better medical care means fewer down days for our warfighters," he said in the agency's own account of the pilot, and a provider at Wilford Hall said that having the notes written meant more time with the patient. [6] As of July, more than 30,000 military clinicians had the tool. [7] If the trial results hold for them, each one gets twenty to thirty minutes of the day back. [4][5]

What becomes of those minutes is the first decision this technology has put to federal health, and the larger ones are arriving by product update. The knee will get its MRI either way. Whether the clinician had time to ask how it got hurt depends on what somebody decides those minutes are for.

Let's roll.
