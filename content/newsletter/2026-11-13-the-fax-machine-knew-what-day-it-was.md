# The Fax Machine Knew What Day It Was

*Every few months, a veteran in Iowa needs VA's permission again to keep seeing his chiropractor. VA's auditors had to read fax cover sheets to learn how long requests like his really wait. Starting in 2027, as each site goes live, new contracts put a deadline on the contractor who will handle them and none on VA.*

Friends,

Every few months, John-Paul Sager's permission to see his chiropractor runs out.

Sager served in the Marine Corps and the Army and lives in Denver, Iowa. The pain in his back and shoulder goes back to his service. The local VA does not offer chiropractic care, so VA pays for his visits to the Vanderloo Chiropractic Clinic, about fifteen miles away in Waterloo. Then the approval lapses and he starts making phone calls, often several. "It seems like it takes entirely too long," he told KFF Health News last year. He values the VA all the same. "You just feel like you're being taken care of by your own," he said. [1]

Linda Gill sees the other half of it. She manages the clinic's office, where about a dozen veterans come through the program. VA pays about $34 a visit, which she called comparable to private insurance, and the paperwork is heavier. The clinic's leaders thought about leaving the VA program and decided to stay. Gill had a plea for the department: "After what they've done for us? Please." [1]

KFF's reporting ends at the clinic door, and so does everything Sager and Gill can see. A request for more care goes into VA, and at some point an answer comes out. On September 24 the inspector general published what happens in between. [2]

The auditors examined about 2.6 million requests for more care, filed from October 2024 through January 2026. The formal channel is VA Form 10-10172, the Request for Services, which a community provider files when a veteran needs more than the original referral covered: more visits, more time, another specialty, a procedure outside the episode VA approved. [3] I call it the second yes. VA's rule gives the department three business days to decide and to tell both the doctor and the veteran. Seventeen percent of the requests had a recorded decision notice within that window. [2]

The detail I cannot put down is how the auditors learned what the real waits were. To check the dates in VA's tracking system, they opened veterans' medical records and read the fax cover sheets. The system had one request at 7 days. The date printed on the fax said 21. It had another at 13 days, and corrected, that one took 35. In a sample of 124 requests at four facilities, the system and the fax disagreed about one time in five. [2]

In that sample, the fax machine kept better time than the tracking system.

My argument is narrow. VA should start one clock the moment a request first arrives, stop it the moment the veteran is told, and publish what it reads. Starting in 2027, as each VA site goes live, ten-year contracts put a deadline on the contractor's stretch of that trip and none on VA's. On January 1, a federal rule requires Medicare Advantage and Medicaid plans to offer the whole thing electronically.

## After it leaves the clinic

At the four facilities the auditors visited, staff said most requests arrive as an electronic fax, and from there the work is done by hand. Someone has to open the fax, sort it, and type it into a tool called the Consult Toolbox before a nurse can review it. The report says requests "can remain in fax queues for up to two months" before that happens. Two facilities each had more than 1,700 waiting to be logged. At a third, managers stopped entering new requests and moved the staff to other authorization work, and by September 2025 more than 3,700 sat unentered. [2]

When a request is finally entered, a person types the date it arrived, because the tool, VHA officials told the auditors, "cannot automatically capture the actual date." The auditors found staff typing one of three things: the date the doctor signed the form, the date the fax was sent, or the date they got to it. There is "no established national procedure for verifying the correctness of receipt dates." [2] If the staff member types the day of entry, a request that sat six weeks in the queue and was decided in two days is a two-day request. My read is that the eight-day average in VA's own data is an estimate, and VA cannot presently say how far off it is.

The people doing this work are short-handed and inventive. At one facility, thirty community care nurses each kept a spreadsheet of their own to track their requests. At another, managers told staff to "close requests on the fourth day after the request was entered into the Consult Toolbox," whether or not a clinician had decided. The notification template was supposed to offer a "needs more time" option. Two staff members said it was gone, which left approval and denial. One of them chose denial and typed a comment: "still under provider review." The veteran was to be advised to follow up with primary care. The practice was still in effect in April. [2]

The inspector general had said as much before. Leigh Ann Searight, a deputy assistant inspector general, told the Senate in February that these delays "can hinder follow-up care for veterans." [4] The new audit added the count: 45 percent decided within three business days, about eight on average, and for 46 percent of requests, about 1.2 million, no record that anyone was told the answer. [2] That is the view from inside VA. From the clinic's side of the fax, it looks different.

## Meanwhile, at the clinic

While the request sits, VA's guidebook tells the clinic what to do, which is nothing: "no additional veteran care should be provided or paid for" until VA sends its notice. A doctor who follows that rule waits, and so does the patient. A doctor who keeps treating is working without an authorization, and the report warns of "potentially leaving the veteran or provider liable for the cost of the care." [2]

Mental health requests waited longest among the specialties the audit broke out, ten days on average, with 68 percent late. Staff at most of the facilities the auditors visited described care delays of "sometimes more than 60 days," and the audit team sent three cases of delayed care to the inspector general's health care inspectors. [2]

Then the answer has to find its way back. A missing record is different from a missing phone call, and some of those 1.2 million doctors and veterans surely heard. But one nurse told the auditors that staff were told to "fax notifications only to providers and not to notify veterans," and VA's own web page for community providers promises a notice to the provider and does not mention the veteran. [2][3]

The provider side has stopped expecting three days. One national therapy network tells its clinicians to send renewals at least 30 days before an authorization expires, because VA "requires a minimum of 30 days to review, approve, and issue a renewed authorization." [5] Some veterans stop asking. Marcus Lewis, a Navy veteran who runs the health center in Park River, North Dakota, put it to KFF this way: "I think a lot of folks just don't want to fight the system." [1]

## Half a clock

On August 27, John Figueroa, signing as Acting Under Secretary for Health, answered the audit in one sentence: "VHA concurs with the recommendations made to the Under Secretary for Health." There are six, each due in July 2027. [2] Four days later VA signed the next generation of its Community Care Network contracts with four companies. The period of performance opens January 1, 2027 and runs through 2036. [6]

I read the contract documents with the audit open beside them: the work statements, the contractor manual, the surveillance plans, and the incentive plan, as released during the competition. They are pre-award versions hosted by a third party, and the task orders could change them, so take what follows as the design on paper. [7][8][9]

The change arrives one site at a time. The contractor takes over these requests "beginning at the start of HCD," the contract's shorthand for health care delivery, and delivery starts station by station as the contracting officer accepts each one, "no sooner than 90 days from contract award." [7] A third-party index of the documents showed no medical task orders posted as of October 11. [6]

When a site does go live, the front door moves for a clinic like Gill's. The request goes into the contractor's portal and gets an identifier and a time. The contractor checks that the package is complete, reviews it against VA's medical policy, and sends VA a recommendation. For the first time there is a deadline in writing: a standard request "shall be reviewed and submitted to VA within one (1) business day of receipt of complete RFS package." An urgent one gets four hours. [7]

Then the deadlines stop. The contractor's day starts only when the package is complete, and a request sent back to the clinic for a missing document has no deadline on the return. Once the recommendation reaches VA, the work statement says "VA has the sole responsibility to make final decisions," and it sets no deadline for the decision and none for telling the veteran. The work statement has a section for exactly this subject, 8.5, "Timeliness of UM Recommendations." [7]

It times the contractor and stops.

The manual's matching section adds nothing. It is marked "RESERVED," the manual's label for any section where it has nothing to add. [8] That is half a clock, and no money rides on the half that exists. The surveillance plans' performance objectives cover network, customer service, claims, medical documentation, and value-based care, with none for these requests, and the incentive plan pays or docks on a count of adverse credit reporting tickets and on value-based network coverage. [9]

The first year is labeled "Establish Baseline." The quarterly report that would show the contractor's numbers begins twelve months after full health care delivery, which VA told the House to expect in 2028. [7][10] The access standard everyone watches starts counting "from the date the referral is approved by VA," so a veteran can wait twelve days for the second yes and four more weeks for the procedure and go into the data as a success. [7]

The documents come close. The contractor's system must record a "Request Received Date/Time" and a "Decision Date/Time" for every request, and the required list has no field for the day the veteran is told. [7] From its first day at each site, the contractor will know what day it is. VA put the contractor's deadline in a contract and left its own in a guidebook.

## What switches on January 1

On the other side of American health care, the date is fixed. Starting January 1, every Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, and CHIP plan must offer an electronic interface that tells a doctor's record system what documentation an approval needs, takes the request, and returns the answer. A doctor may still fax, and the plan must have the electronic door open. Those plans have owed doctors "a specific reason" for any denial since 2026 began, and by the same January date information about the request has to be available to the patient. [11]

The clock is written down too. It "starts when an impacted payer receives a prior authorization request," a request for documents the payer never listed does not pause it, and every plan had to post its approval rate, denial rate, and average and median decision times by March 31 of this year. [12]

Put Gill's clinic in that world for a moment. Before she sends anything, her system asks the payer what the approval requires and fills in the payer's own template. The request goes as a transaction that carries its own timestamp. The answer comes back the same way, with a reason if it is no, and the patient can see it.

Three public HL7 standards built on FHIR do those steps, and insurers covering about 270 million people have pledged to answer at least 80 percent of complete electronic requests in real time by 2027. [13][14] It is a pledge, and one physician in three told the American Medical Association it will make a meaningful difference. [14]

The evidence in hand is older and less glamorous. In 2020, six health plans moved prior authorization for prescriptions and procedures onto electronic tools, and an independent evaluation of more than 40,000 transactions found the median time from request to decision fell from 18.7 hours to 5.7. Fifty-eight percent of experienced users reported sending fewer faxes. [15]

The Department of War does a version of this for its own. The TRICARE Operations Manual requires contractors to decide 90 percent of authorization requests within two business days once a request is complete, and all of them within five. That is a requirement, and I found no public data on performance against it. [16] The TRICARE contractor makes that determination itself, so its deadline covers the decision. The VA contractor's deadline covers the handoff.

VA's portal is real progress measured against a fax queue. It also moves requests on the older transaction, the X12 278, which HHS proposed in April to replace with FHIR for HIPAA covered entities. [8][13] VHA is a covered entity, so a final rule would likely reach these requests, and the solicitation for the new contracts already tells offerors to "consider FHIR as it becomes standardized." [17] VA can build this door once or twice.

## The $1.7 billion objection

The best argument against all of this came from the same inspector general in June. About 25 percent of 1.4 million veterans sent to community care in a single quarter did not meet eligibility requirements, and the auditors estimated VA could have avoided about $1.7 billion over a full year. [18] A program with that finding has every reason to keep a gate, and a comptroller will say so before I finish the sentence. Behind it stands a second worry. Senators have asked large Medicare Advantage insurers for records on AI in coverage decisions, with a lawsuit over one such tool unresolved, because software can speed a no as easily as a yes. [19]

My read is that both risks are real and the wait answers neither. The delay the auditors documented sits in the fax queue before the clinical judgment and in the notice after it. A clock on the handling leaves the judgment alone. In the six-plan evaluation, decisions came more than three times faster and the approval rate moved from 59.9 percent to 60.3. [15] The new contract keeps every final decision with VA and holds the contractor's denial recommendations to a target under 5 percent. [7]

On the software risk, California bars a decision tool from denying, delaying, or modifying care, and Medicare's AI review model leaves every non-payment call to a clinician. [20][21] VA can go one line further: software may approve, and only a clinician may deny.

## Four sentences for section 8.5

The cheapest second yes is the one Sager never has to ask for, and VA has started down that road. In August 2025 it lengthened new community care authorizations to a full year for 30 kinds of care, cardiology, outpatient mental health, oncology, and pain management among them, where some referrals had been reevaluated every 90 to 180 days. "No Veteran should have their health care disrupted by red tape," Secretary Doug Collins said. [22] Chiropractic care is not on the list. Neither is physical therapy as such, or home health. Medicare Advantage sets the wider standard: an approval for a course of treatment "must be valid for as long as medically necessary." [23]

Some asks can be settled before they are written. A VA cardiology clinic that sent about 60 percent of its consults to electronic consultation cut its median wait from 24 days to 13, and AI tools now do a version of that work at the first visit. [24] In a randomized trial of 2,069 patients in China, published in Nature Medicine, a tool that took the history and suggested tests ahead of the specialist shortened consultations by 28.7 percent, and an eight-state U.S. health system says 72 percent of clinicians using its tool reported better workups. [25][26] None of those studies counted repeat approvals, and reviews call the e-consult evidence modest, so I would treat them as tests for VA to run on its own numbers. [27]

Two more are worth borrowing. Exempting what is nearly always approved is harder than it sounds. Texas passed its gold card law in 2021, about 3 percent of physicians had earned one, by the state's count, and Texas rewrote the law in 2025. California's version exempts services with at least a 90 percent approval rate, starting in 2028. [28][29] And approval should stop being the last thing the record shows. Denver Health began tracking whether the specialist's note came back to the referring clinician, and the share rose from 18 percent to 73 percent in two years. [30]

For the paper that still arrives, VA owns the tools. Two of the four facilities the auditors visited had put AI on the fax queue to sort and label requests, and staff said it sped up sorting and caught duplicates. [2] VA's national e-fax tool cut handling time at its Bay Pines medical center from 4.5 minutes a document to 3.1, by VA's account, and its AI inventory lists three more efforts aimed at community care faxes and records. [31][32] Vendors sell the same thing commercially, and one claims a Florida health system's fax-to-transcription time fell from 48 hours to 10 minutes, which is a vendor's account of an unnamed customer. [33] The audit's first recommendation asks VHA to assess automation, "including artificial intelligence-based solutions." [2] That many tools pointed at the same paperwork is an argument for one intake service.

The rest is four sentences, and section 8.5 of the work statement is where they go. The clock starts when the contractor or VA first receives the request. A request for documents that were never on the list does not stop it. It stops when the veteran is told. VA publishes the result every quarter, by facility and by region, split into the contractor's days and VA's.

The Office of Veterans Community Care, which VHA announced in July as the successor to the Office of Integrated Veteran Care, owns the policy, and the contracting officer for the new contracts owns the work statement and the task orders. [2] The surveillance plan says "VA will review the QASP annually at a minimum," which is where the whole-trip number can become a performance objective the two sides share. [9] The first three sentences cost a decision. The fourth goes one step past the audit's sixth recommendation, which asks only for internal oversight. Two other recommendations finish the job: a real pending status, so that a request still under review stops being closed as a denial, and a notice to the veteran every time. [2]

If you run a community care office, you do not have to wait for any of it. Pull ten closed requests, find the fax cover sheets, and compare the dates. The auditors did it 124 times.

## The day he hears

The request VA logged at 13 days took 35. On the other end of it were a doctor's office and a veteran doing what Linda Gill and John-Paul Sager do every few months, which is ask and then wait to hear. [2]

A fax machine wrote down the day the doctor asked. The contract should write down the day the veteran heard.

Gill has already said the rest, in seven words. "After what they've done for us? Please." [1]

Let's roll.
